\section{EvalOpt}
\label{sec:method}

EvalOpt separates whether a revision executes as intended, whether its direction merits further development, and whether its program should replace the global best.
Disagreement-based preference mining selects the human feedback used by search.
Depth-first evaluator evolution then retains a correction hypothesis across revisions, including unsuccessful attempts whose execution records inform the next test.
Execution checks establish what ran; Train evidence governs bounded exploration and rollback; sealed Val comparisons govern global retention.
This separation permits intermediate implementations without treating them as confirmed improvements.

\subsection{Setup and revision space}
\label{sec:problem}
\label{sec:measurement}
\label{sec:runtime}

Starting from a seed evaluator $E_0$, EvalOpt revises an executable evaluation program $E_\theta$ using human feedback, with the base model $M$ fixed.
The program has three editable stages: analysis $A$ prepares task and artifact evidence, judgment $J$ applies criteria through LLM agents and tools, and synthesis $S$ combines their findings into scores and critiques.
Their nodes, prompts, tool interfaces, and evidence routes form the revision space.
A candidate is a complete program, including any coordinated changes across stages; component probes diagnose changes, but confirmation requires end-to-end execution.

For a task $x$, the evaluator scores each output $y$ separately, without seeing the competing output or human preference.
Within a domain $D$ sharing an evaluation standard, the objective is agreement with human preferences on unseen inputs,
\begin{equation}
 \max_{\theta}\ J_D(\theta),\qquad
 J_D(\theta)=
 \Pr_{(x,y^+,y^-)\sim\mathcal P_D,\,\xi}
 \bigl[\bar s_\theta(x,y^+)>\bar s_\theta(x,y^-)\bigr].
 \label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
Here $y^+$ is the human-preferred output, $\mathcal P_D$ is the domain distribution, and $\bar s_\theta$ is the score under a scoring protocol frozen before search with execution randomness $\xi$.
Before mining, a fixed-seed random split assigns original-input groups from the frozen domain source pool to Train, Val, and Test.
Mining and random-anchor selection operate only within Train.
Val and Test inputs and outputs remain fixed, with compared methods sharing the same split in each run (Appendix~\ref{app:measurement}).
Train exposes artifacts, human feedback, and traces to the optimizer.
Val cases and traces remain sealed; the optimizer receives only the promotion indicator and retained best score.
Test stays locked until search ends and the selected program is frozen (Appendix~\ref{app:measurement}).

\subsection{Disagreement-based preference mining}
\label{sec:mining}

Mining prioritizes disagreements among metrics and judges, while retaining a random anchor to check for errors shared by the available signals.
The Train pool contains pairs $r=(x,y_a,y_b)$ from existing datasets or newly generated outputs.
Post-split output generation for mining is restricted to the assigned Train inputs.
Each signal $j$ yields $u_j(r)\in\{-1,0,+1,\bot\}$: preference for $y_b$, a tie, preference for $y_a$, or unavailable evidence.
Each sign is obtained by comparing that signal's scores using a prespecified tie margin.
Signals sharing a model or underlying metric belong to a source group.
Each signal is either overall or mapped to one or more quality dimensions $q$ from the domain's evaluation standard.
These definitions are fixed before revealing human preferences.

Within each dimension, a source group contributes at most one sign; opposing signs within the group yield abstention.
The dimension's aggregate preference is the sign of the sum of group votes.
We rank pairs by
\begin{equation}
 P(r)=2\sum_q I_{\mathrm{within},q}(r)
      +\sum_q I_{\mathrm{overall},q}(r)
      +I_{\mathrm{tradeoff}}(r).
 \label{eq:conflict}
\end{equation}
The first indicator marks opposing signals from different source groups on dimension $q$; the second marks opposition between an overall signal and a signal on $q$.
The last is one when aggregate preferences disagree across dimensions.
Ties and missing signals never count as opposition.
The fixed 2:1 weighting prioritizes disagreement about the same criterion, while the capped trade-off term prioritizes cross-dimension conflicts.
The score allocates annotation priority to these predefined forms of disagreement.

Before computing mining scores, original-input hashes select the random anchor, keeping outputs from the same input together.
The anchor uses part of the feedback budget.
The remainder is filled by decreasing $P(r)$, with pair identifiers breaking ties.
Signal inventories, tie margins, and anchor size are fixed before selected labels are revealed (Appendix~\ref{app:annotation}).
After selection, we obtain human preferences by reuse or blinded annotation.
Reuse requires the same task and output pair and matching evaluation criteria and annotation protocol, or an explicitly audited harmonization.
Otherwise, annotation supplies new preferences.
Both routes preserve label provenance and missing feedback fields; unselected labels remain hidden.
We evaluate mining by downstream evaluator improvement, not disagreement yield.

\subsection{Depth-first evaluator evolution}
\label{sec:proposal}
\label{sec:search}

The controller maintains an \emph{anchor} $R$, a \emph{working program} $W$, and the Val-selected \emph{best program} $B$.
The anchor is the direction's initial reference, then its most recently confirmed program; working is the program being explored and may lack evidence of final benefit.
A new direction starts with $R=W=B$ and records a target failure pattern, a proposed correction, and diagnostic history.
Refining its implementation continues the direction; replacing the correction opens another direction.
While active, the direction receives the next revision or diagnostic until an explicit ending condition applies.
The direction is the unit of continued exploration; the complete program is the unit of global selection.

\paragraph{Declare the experiment, then check execution.}
Before running a candidate, the agent commits to five items: affected $A/J/S$ nodes, a predicted intermediate change, Train cases expected to improve or remain correct, a direct-improvement or scaffolding type, and observable acceptance and exit conditions.
A scaffolding step also names its next integration step and a finite exploration window.
Execution checks inspect the effective configuration, actual node calls, input/output contracts, and produced evidence.
These checks distinguish a tool that did not run from one that ran without the predicted effect; neither conclusion is inferred from accuracy alone.
Observed records remain separate from the agent's interpretation and survive rejection.

\paragraph{Compare programs under matched conditions.}
Each paired comparison between parent and candidate fixes cases, scoring rules, budgets, and inputs outside the intervention.
A synthesis edit reuses analysis and judgment evidence where valid; a judgment edit fixes analysis; an analysis edit fixes downstream implementations.
These probes locate behavioral changes, while end-to-end runs test whether they survive the full pipeline.
Each direction also keeps a fixed Train panel $\mathcal D_d$ of targets and counterexamples.
New Train coverage cases test changes outside that panel, but do not replace its reference trajectory.
Before updating $R$, the controller compares the candidate against $R$ on this panel and checks fresh coverage.
The panel remains learning data, not independent validation.

Let $\widehat p_i(E)$ be the observed frequency of a correct preference ordering under the frozen scoring protocol, reducing to a binary outcome for one execution.
For a prespecified cohort $\mathcal C$, the paired gain is
\begin{equation}
 \widehat g_{\mathcal C}(E,F)
 =\frac{1}{|\mathcal C|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal C}
       [\widehat p_i(E)-\widehat p_i(F)].
 \label{eq:paired-gain}
\end{equation}
Counterexample losses, fixed-scale margins, evidence coverage, ranking instability, and execution cost are recorded separately.
Margins can expose progress before a ranking flips, but cannot replace the preference objective.
A fixed initial measurement is followed by symmetric parent--candidate repeats only when a decision remains uncertain, under prespecified stopping and cost limits.
An exhausted comparison is unresolved, not a reason to rerun until improvement appears.

\paragraph{Confirm, stage, or roll back on Train.}
A candidate becomes the new anchor only after execution and mechanism checks pass, end-to-end gain against $R$ is supported, and counterexample losses meet fixed tolerances.
The controller then sets $R=W=C$.
If benefit is uncertain, it retains $R$ and may stage a valid candidate as $W$ for bounded testing.
Scaffolding can likewise enter $W$ after its intermediate checks pass, without being declared an improvement.
For example, $J$ can add and verify a browser tool before a later edit teaches $S$ to consume its evidence.
Staging requires a declared next step, a cost cap, a deadline, and a temporary-loss tolerance.
Confirmed degradation, failed integration, or an expired window returns $W$ to $R$; diagnostics remain available.
An execution failure instead enters bounded implementation repair, not automatic rejection of the correction hypothesis.
Appendix~\ref{app:direction-decisions} gives the decision conditions.

\paragraph{Adapt the permitted scope, not a proxy for semantic distance.}
The trust-region idea~\citep{conn2000trust} controls which modifications are allowed around the anchor: a local node edit, a structural edit within one component, or a coordinated change across components.
Character counts and calls remain resource bounds, not a semantic metric.
Expansion requires a prespecified history of reliable mechanism predictions, confirmed gains, and controlled counterexample losses.
An ineffective interface calls for repair; a functioning but persistently harmful mechanism calls for rollback and narrower scope or direction termination.
Noise calls for limited additional measurement, not mechanically shorter prompts.

We retain $\rho$ only as an auxiliary observed-to-predicted gain ratio.
It is available when a positive numerical prediction was frozen before execution, exceeds a preset floor, matches the measured metric and cohort, and has sufficient measurement precision.
Otherwise $\rho$ is unavailable; scaffolding without a numerical gain prediction is not assigned a zero denominator or automatic exemption.
Neither a single high ratio nor a long patch triggers expansion.
Direction and staging budgets bound exploration; exhaustion without decisive evidence is recorded as unresolved.
These rules define trust-region-inspired scope control, not a convergence guarantee for a stochastic trust-region algorithm (Appendix~\ref{app:algorithm}).

\paragraph{Promote the global best only on Val.}
At prespecified checkpoints, an execution-valid $W$ is compared with $B$ on the complete frozen Val cohort.
Promotion requires the fixed Val improvement criterion; missing outcomes cannot reduce the denominator.
Only the promotion indicator and retained best score are released, following the motivation of restricted leaderboard feedback~\citep{blum2015ladder}.
Val does not update the Train anchor, repair budget, or direction-ending decision, and promotion does not establish the cause of an improvement.
After the final checkpoint, search returns $B$ for one locked Test assessment.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:era} and Appendix~\ref{app:algorithm} specify confirmation, staging, measurement, and rollback.
