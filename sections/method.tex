\section{IterEval}
\label{sec:method}

IterEval separates \emph{candidate acceptance} from \emph{direction continuation}.
A candidate that fails to improve agreement may reveal an ineffective correction, an incomplete implementation, or repairs offset by regressions.
Its rejection therefore does not, by itself, decide whether to try another direction.
The direction is the unit of continued exploration; the complete program is the unit of selection.

\subsection{Setup and revision space}
\label{sec:problem}
\label{sec:measurement}
\label{sec:runtime}

Starting from a seed evaluator $E_0$, IterEval revises an executable evaluation program $E_\theta$ using human feedback, with the base model $M$ fixed.
The program specifies artifact adapters $A$, rubrics and dimensions $K$, evaluator modules $V$, tools $T$, evidence routing $\Pi$, and fusion $G$.
A revision can change several connected components, such as a detector, its route, and the scoring instruction that uses its findings.
These edits produce one complete candidate program, which is executed and evaluated as a whole.
Figure~\ref{fig:runtime} details the component interfaces.

For a task $x$, the evaluator scores each output $y$ separately, without seeing the competing output or human preference.
Within a domain $D$ sharing an evaluation standard, the objective is agreement with human preferences on unseen inputs,
\begin{equation}
 \max_{\theta}\ J_D(\theta),\qquad
 J_D(\theta)=
 \Pr_{(x,y^+,y^-)\sim\mathcal P_D,\,\xi}
 \bigl[\bar s_\theta(x,y^+)>\bar s_\theta(x,y^-)\bigr].
 \label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
Here $y^+$ is the human-preferred output, $\mathcal P_D$ is the domain distribution, and $\bar s_\theta$ is the score under a fixed repeated-scoring protocol with execution randomness $\xi$.
Disagreement-based preference mining determines which feedback to acquire; depth-first evaluator evolution determines how to act on that feedback.
The proposal subset $\Dprop$ exposes artifacts, human feedback, and execution traces; the disjoint checking subset $\Ddev$ returns only aggregate candidate measurements.
Both are development data, separate from the final held-out assessment (Appendix~\ref{app:measurement}).

\subsection{Disagreement-based preference mining}
\label{sec:mining}

Mining prioritizes comparisons that expose disagreement among existing metrics and judges, while retaining random comparisons to check for shared errors.
The pool contains output pairs $r=(x,y_a,y_b)$ from existing datasets or newly generated outputs, grouped by their original input.
Each signal $j$ yields $u_j(r)\in\{-1,0,+1,\bot\}$ by comparing its own scores under a prespecified tie margin.
The signs indicate preference for $y_b$, a tie, preference for $y_a$, or unavailable evidence, respectively.
Signal definitions, source groups, and mappings to quality dimensions are fixed before revealing human preferences.

We distinguish opposing signals on the same dimension, opposition between overall and dimensional judgments, and trade-offs between dimensions.
Their indicators define the ranking score
\begin{equation}
 P(r)=2\sum_q I_{\mathrm{within},q}(r)
      +\sum_q I_{\mathrm{overall},q}(r)
      +I_{\mathrm{tradeoff}}(r).
 \label{eq:conflict}
\end{equation}
The first indicator requires opposing signals from different source groups on dimension $q$; the second requires an overall signal to oppose a signal on $q$.
The last indicator is one when dimension-level preferences disagree, with at most one vote per source group in each dimension's aggregate.
Within-dimension conflicts receive twice the weight of overall--dimension conflicts; the trade-off term adds one to the priority.
Ties and missing signals do not count as opposing preferences.

A prespecified random anchor is selected by original-input hashes before scoring.
The remaining comparisons are ranked by decreasing $P(r)$, with pair identifiers breaking ties, and selected up to the feedback budget.
The anchor and targeted selections share this budget.
Signal inventories, tie margins, and anchor size are domain-specific settings; Appendix~\ref{app:annotation} specifies their recording and the source-group aggregation.
For selected comparisons, compatible human preferences are reused when available; otherwise, blinded annotation supplies the missing preferences.
Both routes preserve each label's source, artifacts, signals, and execution records.
Unselected labels remain hidden from evolution.
The relevant endpoint is subsequent evaluator improvement, not the amount of disagreement collected.

\subsection{Depth-first evaluator evolution}
\label{sec:proposal}
\label{sec:search}

Candidate selection asks \emph{which program to retain}; direction continuation asks \emph{what to test next} (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}b).
We formulate this search as sequential program optimization with persistent directions.
The search keeps a best program $B$, a working program $W$ to revise, an active direction $d$, and its diagnostic record $z$.
Selection updates $B$ using checking agreement.
Unsuccessful trials still enter $z$ and can inform another revision or probe within $d$.

\paragraph{Choosing a direction.}
When no direction is active, the agent examines proposal errors and earlier trials, then states the error to address, its proposed explanation, and the intended correction.
For example, overlooked layout faults may suggest that relevant visual evidence is missing or unused, motivating a correction that makes those faults count in scoring.
This description defines the semantic scope of continued work, with $W=B$ at its start.
The agent is instructed to refine implementations and unresolved explanations within that scope, and to end the direction before pursuing a different correction.
A persistent identifier links the description to its trials and diagnostics.

\paragraph{Revising and diagnosing.}
The agent revises $W$ to produce a candidate $C$, recording the actual edits, their intent, and the inspected proposal cases.
Every valid changed candidate is evaluated on the same checking set.
Proposal-case traces show what the program did, which errors were repaired, and which correct judgments regressed relative to its actual parent.
The diagnostic record separates these observed outcomes from the agent's explanations and remaining questions.
The same zero gain can mean unchanged orderings or offsetting repairs and regressions.
Neither outcome alone reveals whether the intended correction was implemented.

\paragraph{Choosing the next test.}
The agent uses $z$ to ask whether the intended behavior occurred, whether it repaired the target error, and what else changed.
In Figure~\ref{fig:overview}b, layout errors are detected but final scores stay unchanged.
Testing whether the findings reach the scoring step addresses an unresolved implementation question within the same direction.
If the intended behavior already occurs without repairing the error, the next test instead examines the correction or its strength.
If repairs introduce regressions, contrast cases test where the correction should apply.
Supported probes compare parent and edit with upstream inputs fixed; changes to observations or routing require complete execution.
Probe outcomes enter $z$, but only a candidate's checking result can update $B$.

\paragraph{Selecting the best program.}
At trial $t$, acceptance depends on recorded checking agreement $\widehat J_{\mathrm{dev}}$,
\begin{equation}
 B_{t+1}=\begin{cases}
 C_t,&\widehat J_{\mathrm{dev}}(C_t)>\widehat J_{\mathrm{dev}}(B_t)
       \ \text{and}\ \Gamma_t(C_t),\\
 B_t,&\text{otherwise}.
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:acceptance}
\end{equation}
Here $\Gamma_t$ requires complete, comparable execution, nondecreasing coverage, and the prespecified worst-stratum regression check.
This rule preserves the recorded best development agreement without deciding direction continuation.

Within a continuing direction, the next revision need not retain the rejected candidate's code.
Using $C_t$ as the next parent requires complete, comparable local and checking outcomes against both parent and best program.
Given those measurements, $C_t$ can be retained if it is accepted, causes no observed damage, or qualifies for a limited exploratory step.
The last case requires previously uncredited progress: a newly measured repair of an error present when the direction began, or a predeclared intermediate-field change, with no regression on designated protection cases.
Otherwise, the next parent is the pre-update best $B_t$.
Incomplete local evidence can force this fallback even for an accepted candidate, without reversing acceptance or discarding diagnostics.
The parent rule protects program state while retaining failed trials as information; Appendix~\ref{app:algorithm} gives the exact checks.

\paragraph{Continuing before branching.}
While a direction remains active, the next revision or probe uses its accumulated diagnostic record.
To end it, the agent submits an explicit reason, with instructions to identify the unresolved question and why no different informative test is ready.
This reason may concern an implemented correction that fails to address the target errors, or remaining implementation questions with no useful next test.
After an ending, the next session starts a new direction from the current best program.
Rejection or parent fallback alone cannot trigger this transition.
Depth-first evolution denotes this ordering of search decisions, with failed candidates retained as evidence for continued work.
The agent judges semantic continuity and when to end; the controller preserves the history and applies program-selection rules, without guaranteeing a minimum depth or a correct diagnosis.
Resource or execution stops leave the direction unresolved.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:era} and Appendix~\ref{app:algorithm} specify the loop and its execution controls.
Normal completion returns the best program selected on development data, fixed before independent assessment.
