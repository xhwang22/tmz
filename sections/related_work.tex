\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Learning evaluation procedures from human feedback.}
Reward hacking exposes a limitation of improving generators against an imperfect evaluator: optimization can increase evaluator scores by exploiting errors in how quality is assessed \citep{wang2026rewardhacking}.
This motivates using human feedback to improve the evaluation procedure itself, rather than treating it as a fixed objective.
We share the goal of aligning judgments with human preferences with learned reward models and trained judges \citep{xu2023imagereward,kim2024prometheus,ke2024critiquellm}.
Evaluator fine-tuning changes model weights, whereas our search changes an executable evaluation program with the base model fixed.
Criteria-learning methods, including preference-grounded query-specific rubrics, are closer to our approach because they use human feedback to develop explicit evaluation rules \citep{shankar2024spade,liu2024autocalibrate,shankar2024evalgen,lv2026queryrubrics}.
Our search also changes how evidence is gathered and how it affects the final judgment, allowing a correction to span several steps of the evaluation procedure.
Tool-based inspection and evolving verifier skills already appear in Agent-as-a-Judge and WebGrader \citep{zhuge2025agentjudge,chen2026webgrader}; our question is how to adapt that procedure to human quality trade-offs that execution checks alone do not specify.

\paragraph{Continuing directions in program evolution.}
Our depth-first evaluator evolution builds on methods for revising prompts, modules, and agent code \citep{khattab2024dspy,yuksekgonul2025textgrad,agrawal2026gepa,hu2025adas,zhang2025dgm}.
Its focus is how search proceeds after a candidate fails to improve the score.
SkillOpt already uses a rejected-edit buffer to inform later proposals while accepting only score-improving edits \citep{yang2026skillopt}.
Our search makes a further distinction between retaining that history, continuing the same direction, and inheriting the rejected implementation.
An eligible candidate can remain the working program without becoming the best program, and a direction can remain active even when inheritance fails.

Persistent search is likewise not unique to IterEval: HarnessBank maintains distinct harnesses, and SkillHEX uses executable hypothesis tests and a persistent revision tree \citep{luo2026harnessbank,feng2026skillhex}.
SkillHEX addresses similarly ambiguous failures through test-time branch selection under sparse terminal rewards, without training or validation sets.
Our depth-first evaluator evolution instead allocates successive revisions to an active direction, subject to diagnostic and budget checks, while measuring complete evaluation programs against a development cohort of human preferences.
SkillLens's distinction between extracting a skill and successfully consuming it reinforces the need to examine executed behavior, not merely the plausibility of a proposed revision \citep{huang2026skillconsumption}.
Our comparison tests whether depth-first direction continuation improves evaluator alignment over alternative search allocations under matched budgets.

\paragraph{Mining preferences for evaluator evolution.}
Disagreement-based preference mining follows active preference learning in allocating limited human feedback selectively, with disagreement-based sampling rooted in query-by-committee \citep{seung1992committee,muldrew2024activepreference,oh2025activepreference}.
It applies this principle to evaluator evolution by reducing the priority of repeated comparisons on which existing evaluation signals agree, while retaining a random anchor for shared blind spots.
Unlike Benchmark$^2$, which selects test sets to preserve ranking consistency, discriminability, and capability alignment \citep{qian2026benchmark2}, our preference mining selects comparisons to improve the evaluator rather than measure competing models.
Existing datasets can supply both comparisons and reusable preferences; new annotation fills missing feedback.
The relevant endpoint is the resulting evaluator's agreement at matched human cost, rather than the selected set's disagreement or discriminability.
