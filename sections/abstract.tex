Evaluating open-ended outputs requires task-dependent judgments over multiple, often competing, aspects of quality.
Existing proxy metrics and model-based evaluators remain imperfect on such judgments and do not consistently align with human preferences.
Humans can often tell which of two concrete outputs is better, but it is much harder to turn that ability into a comprehensive and effective evaluation procedure.
So we study evaluator self-evolution by treating the evaluation procedure itself as the object of iterative improvement from human preferences.
Existing evolution methods largely leave each next revision to the optimizer model based on previous outcomes.
Yet on complex evaluation tasks, this often leads evolution away from a reasonable direction after an early failure, even when that direction may simply be underdeveloped.
This suggests that an early failure may reflect incomplete implementation rather than a flawed direction.
We introduce \ERA{}, a depth-first evolution method that uses diagnostic results to guide continued work on a direction within a fixed search budget.
ERA separates candidate acceptance from direction continuation, allowing failed attempts to guide further implementation without replacing the best evaluator found so far.
We hypothesize that ERA yields closer agreement with human preferences and more sustained improvement under matched search budgets.
Downstream comparisons will test whether the evolved evaluators improve output selection and critic-guided refinement.
On representative tasks, we will also test whether using their scores as training rewards improves generation quality.
