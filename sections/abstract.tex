Automated evaluators for open-ended tasks often miss the task-dependent trade-offs reflected in human preferences.
Human preferences identify better outputs in concrete comparisons, but do not specify an evaluation procedure for new ones.
We study how an evaluation agent can evolve this procedure from human feedback.
Doing so requires acquiring informative preferences and turning the problems they reveal into working changes to the evaluation procedure.
A limited annotation budget can be spent on repetitive comparisons that provide little guidance for improving the evaluator.
Disagreement-based preference acquisition allocates fewer annotations to comparisons on which existing evaluation signals agree.
In the iterative optimizer we tested, unsuccessful candidates were repeatedly followed by a return to the best evaluator and proposals in other directions.
Yet a direction may require coordinated changes across the evaluation procedure before it produces a gain.
We introduce \ERA{}, falsification-guided depth-first evolution that separates accepting a candidate from continuing its direction.
A failed candidate does not by itself refute a direction's hypothesis.
ERA checks whether the proposed behavior occurred before treating its outcome as a test of the hypothesis.
Diagnostic results guide further implementation or support refutation.
Our cross-domain evaluation design tests acquisition and evolution separately and end-to-end under matched annotation and search budgets.
Quantitative displays are simulated; confirmatory evaluation remains pending.
