Automated evaluation remains difficult to align with human preferences on
open-ended tasks. Quality depends on context and trade-offs among criteria,
making human judgments difficult to express as an interpretable, executable
evaluation procedure. Existing metrics and model-based judges offer useful
approximations, but their disagreement with people leaves open how the procedure
itself should change. We study evaluation-agent self-evolution from human
feedback. Rather than fitting only the parameters of a predefined scorer, our
method, \ERA{}, evolves its evaluation criteria, evidence acquisition, and
judgment procedures with the base model fixed. The approach connects two
problems. Semantic disagreement among existing evaluation signals guides the
acquisition of structured human feedback. That feedback then grounds hypotheses
about evaluation failures, which ERA tests through connected changes to the
executable program. Hypotheses guide exploration; complete programs are selected
against separate human feedback. Reused and newly collected preferences supply
the same learning problem, with separate provenance and annotation-quality
reporting. Across structured visual artifacts, images and 3D, text generation,
and automated research, the study design compares program evolution with
evaluator fine-tuning and score calibration. It separately tests alignment and
selection, critic-guided refinement, and planned reward-guided training.
Quantitative displays are simulated; alignment gains and advantages over
parameter tuning remain to be established.
