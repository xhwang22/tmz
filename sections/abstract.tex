Automated evaluators for open-ended tasks often miss the task-dependent trade-offs reflected in human preferences.
Human feedback records judgments on concrete outputs, but does not specify an evaluation procedure for new ones.
We study whether an evaluation agent can learn such a procedure by evolving how it gathers evidence and makes judgments.
To obtain feedback for evolution, we use disagreement-based preference acquisition to reduce annotation of comparisons on which existing evaluation signals agree.
Even with human feedback, search in the optimization traces we examined repeatedly discarded unsuccessful candidates and moved to other directions.
Yet an evaluation procedure may need several connected changes before a direction produces a gain.
We introduce \ERA{}, falsification-guided depth-first evolution that separates accepting a candidate from continuing its direction.
ERA uses failures to guide further implementation without replacing the best evaluator with an unsuccessful candidate.
Continuation depends on new evidence, distinguishing implementation obstacles from evidence against the direction's hypothesis.
Our cross-domain evaluation protocol compares annotation efficiency, search progress, and agreement with human preferences under matched budgets.
Quantitative displays are simulated; confirmatory evaluation remains pending.
