\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

An automatically generated slide deck can preserve the source facts yet obscure
their relationships through poor visual hierarchy. A reconstructed webpage can
resemble a reference image while failing to implement a required interaction.
These examples illustrate a difficulty in evaluating structured artifacts:
different quality judgments require different evidence, and an overall preference
depends on how those judgments are combined. Recent benchmarks for presentations
and front-end generation make such evaluation requirements explicit
\citep{pptagent2025,presentbench2026,si2025design2code}.

Language-model evaluators provide a flexible alternative to fixed similarity
metrics, but their agreement with people depends on the instructions, evidence,
and decision procedure they receive
\citep{liu2023geval,zheng2023judging,shankar2024evalgen}.
Human feedback can identify a preferred artifact without identifying why an
evaluator disagreed. A missing observation, an inappropriate criterion, and an
incorrect trade-off can produce the same ranking error. Merely increasing the
weight of a criterion does not resolve all three causes.

We study the following question: \emph{can human overall preferences guide the
revision of an executable evaluator, including how it acquires and combines
evidence?} We formulate the evaluator as a structured program and introduce
\ERA{} as the method for revising it. The program exposes six connected components: artifact
representation, rubric, specialist evaluators, evidence tools, evidence routing,
and synthesis. An outer revision process uses authorized training examples and
execution traces to identify a candidate mechanism of error and propose a
corresponding program change. The deployed program remains a pointwise evaluator:
it receives one artifact and its task context, not the human comparison used to
develop it.

Figure~\ref{fig:overview} separates training supervision, program revision, and
use of the frozen evaluator. The same revision method can be instantiated under
different evaluation standards, without assuming that one learned scorer serves
all domains.

Reflective optimization and automated agent design support revisions to prompts,
workflows, and code
\citep{agrawal2026gepa,hu2025adas,zhang2025aflow,zhang2025dgm}.
Our focus is the evaluator-specific learning problem: overall preference provides
supervision, execution evidence supports revision, and independent artifact scores
must preserve useful quality distinctions. The central hypothesis is that
revising evidence acquisition and synthesis can address errors that remain under
prompt-only adaptation. This is an empirical hypothesis, not an assumed benefit
of a larger search space.

The paper develops three elements: (i) a structured formulation of the
evaluator-revision problem; (ii) an evidence-guided search procedure with separate
proposal, measurement, and allocation decisions; and (iii) a controlled protocol
for assessing preference agreement, artifact selection, and feedback-guided
refinement.
