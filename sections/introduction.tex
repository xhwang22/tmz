\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Open-ended generation exposes a gap between what automated evaluators measure
and what people value. A fluent summary may mislead, an appealing image may
violate its request, and an original research proposal may fail to test its
hypothesis. Metrics and model-based judges scale assessment, but studies of
summarization, image preference, and model-based
judging document limitations in their agreement with human judgments
\citep{fabbri2021summeval,xu2023imagereward,zheng2023judging}.
Improving generation against these evaluators therefore need not improve the
qualities that matter to people.

The difficulty is making human preferences explicit enough to automate.
Open-ended quality has no complete deterministic verifier. Criteria depend on
the task, and even an agreed list of criteria leaves their interpretation and
trade-offs unresolved. Visual similarity, for example, is central to screenshot
reconstruction, whereas specification-conditioned web generation permits
different appearances that satisfy the same requirements. An overall preference
records a decision without fully specifying how to make such distinctions on
new outputs. Aligning the evaluation criteria themselves is consequently part
of the learning problem \citep{shankar2024evalgen}.

We study whether human preference data can drive the self-evolution of an
evaluation agent toward a task's evaluation standard. Existing evaluation
knowledge supplies initial criteria and tools; human feedback supplies evidence
about where their current use falls short. Our method, \ERA{}, evolves the
executable evaluation program while keeping its base model fixed
(Figure~\ref{fig:overview}). The human standard remains the target. What evolves
is the agent's explicit approximation of that standard, including how it obtains
evidence and turns that evidence into judgments.

Why evolve the program rather than only tune parameters? Fine-tuning can improve
judgment, as rubric-conditioned evaluators illustrate
\citep{kim2024prometheus,kim2024prometheus2}. With a fixed observation path,
however, tuning how evidence is interpreted leaves the evidence available to
the judge unchanged. Calibration can adjust existing scores without acquiring
new evidence.
Program evolution exposes criteria, tools, and evidence use as objects that can
be changed and tested. Fine-tuning can also learn criteria and tool use when its
training interface permits them. A shared-observation control therefore separates
changes to available evidence from changes to its interpretation. An explicit
program is not automatically a faithful explanation. We therefore
include evaluator fine-tuning and calibration as alternatives, without assuming
an alignment or efficiency advantage (Appendix~\ref{app:adaptation}).

Preference-guided evolution requires both informative feedback and a way to act
on it. We map existing signals to quality dimensions before inspecting labels,
separating disagreement about one criterion from trade-offs between criteria.
We hypothesize that disagreements among reliable, nonredundant signals can make
a fixed annotation budget more informative than random sampling. Available
dimensional judgments and localized errors then inform hypotheses about missing
observations or inappropriate interpretation. For example, a stricter
faithfulness criterion cannot assess a source qualification that the program
never makes available to the judge. We hypothesize that such dependencies
between evidence acquisition and judgment favor coordinated program evolution.
ERA uses hypotheses as units of exploration and complete programs as units of
selection, allowing a connected mechanism to be tested without equating one
unsuccessful implementation with a refuted hypothesis.

Reusing existing preferences and collecting new judgments instantiate this same
learning problem. Their provenance, agreement, and abstention remain separately
reported; building a corpus does not establish the value of its sampling policy.
The scope spans four domain families, with a separate evaluator for each
evaluation standard. We distinguish alignment and selection on fixed candidates
(C1), critic-guided refinement (C2), and planned reward-guided training (C3).
Closer ranking agreement alone cannot establish useful feedback for generation.
