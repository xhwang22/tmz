\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

An appealing image may omit a requested object. A fluent summary may remove a
qualification that changes the source's meaning. A research proposal may offer an
interesting hypothesis without an experiment capable of testing it. These are
evaluation problems in open-ended generation: multiple outputs are reasonable,
quality has several dimensions, and no single deterministic verifier defines the
complete ordering. Existing metrics and model-based judges provide useful but
different evidence about that ordering
\citep{fabbri2021summeval,xu2023imagereward,ghosh2023geneval,liu2023geval}.

The central difficulty is that a preference error does not identify its cause.
A summary evaluator might never inspect the source qualification, observe it
without passing it to the judge, or judge faithfulness correctly but give fluency
too much weight. The same wrong ordering is compatible with each mechanism.
Adding another score does not resolve this ambiguity when sources measure
different constructs or share a blind spot. Work on model-based judging and
human-authored criteria motivates examining what supports a judgment
\citep{zheng2023judging,shankar2024evalgen}. The resulting question is:
\emph{given a task and its human feedback, can we evolve an evaluator that better
follows that task's evaluation standard}?

We introduce \PtwoE{}, Preference-Guided Evaluator Evolution, for domains with
open-ended outputs and an existing evaluation ecosystem.
Figure~\ref{fig:overview} presents the complete framework. \PtwoE{} maps available
signals to quality dimensions before exposing human labels. Suitable existing
preferences can be reused; tasks without them require new human judgments.
These are two data-construction settings for the same algorithmic problem,
not different definitions of the method. Human evidence has
two roles: it helps locate evaluation gaps and supplies the standard against
which candidate mechanisms are selected. Within P2E, \ERA{} is our method for
evidence-guided evaluator evolution. With the base model fixed, it evolves how
an executable program represents artifacts, acquires observations, and turns
them into judgments. The object of learning is the evaluation procedure itself.

Two hypotheses motivate the design. First, disagreements between reliable,
nonredundant signals may make a fixed annotation budget more informative than
uniform sampling. Second, failures that depend on acquiring, routing, and
interpreting evidence may require coordinated evolution of those components.
A new observation cannot affect a judgment unless it reaches a module able to
interpret it. ERA therefore organizes variation around mechanism hypotheses
while selecting complete programs. A failed implementation need not refute its
hypothesis, and continuation need not promote the current program. These
distinctions motivate matched-budget comparisons with independent proposals
and component-restricted search.

A common evolution procedure is instantiated separately for each evaluation
standard. We distinguish agreement and selection on fixed candidates (C1),
refinement through a frozen critic (C2), and training under a frozen reward (C3).
Success at one level does not establish success at the next. This paper develops
the feedback-mining formulation, ERA evolution procedure, and controlled studies
needed to test these hypotheses in both data-construction settings. The ability
to build a new preference corpus and the value of a particular sampling policy
require different evidence.
