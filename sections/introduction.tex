\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Evaluating open-ended outputs is difficult because their quality depends on task-specific criteria and trade-offs.
A shorter summary may preserve the central argument better than a longer one, while an elaborate webpage may still fail its main purpose.
Metrics and model-based judges capture useful aspects of quality, yet do not consistently match human preferences \citep{fabbri2021summeval,xu2023imagereward,zheng2023judging}.
Collecting more evaluation signals does not by itself resolve the central question: which evidence should matter, and how should conflicting criteria be weighed for this task?

Human preferences provide feedback on which output is better, but leave open what an evaluator should inspect and how its findings should affect future judgments.
We study how an evaluation agent can evolve an evaluation procedure from human feedback by changing how it gathers evidence and makes judgments.
Prior work uses human feedback to refine evaluation criteria or select evaluation functions \citep{liu2024autocalibrate,shankar2024evalgen}, while agentic judges gather evidence with tools \citep{zhuge2025agentjudge}.
Methods that propose and test changes to prompts and agent code provide a starting point for evolving this procedure \citep{agrawal2026gepa,zhang2025dgm}.
Evolving the procedure requires two linked steps: acquiring informative preferences and turning the problems they reveal into working changes.

Obtaining informative preferences is difficult in a new domain.
Hand-designed comparisons can embed the designer's assumptions; unrestricted collection can produce repetitive judgments.
Our disagreement-based preference acquisition allocates fewer annotations to comparisons on which existing evaluation signals agree, drawing on query-by-committee and active preference learning \citep{seung1992committee,muldrew2024activepreference}.
Random sampling checks for shared blind spots among these signals.
We test whether these preferences support greater evaluator improvement than random sampling at equal human annotation cost.

Even with informative preferences, search can leave a direction unfinished.
In the iterative optimizer we tested, candidates needed to improve the development score to replace the best evaluator.
After rejections, search repeatedly returned to that evaluator and proposed other directions instead of further developing the rejected implementation.
This pattern matters when a direction requires coordinated changes across the evaluation procedure.
For example, evaluating omissions in a summary requires detecting missing content, judging its importance, and making that judgment affect the final score.
Changing only one step may leave the overall judgment unchanged.

Rejecting a candidate need not end work on its direction.
\ERA{} separates \emph{candidate acceptance} from \emph{direction continuation} through falsification-guided depth-first evolution (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}c).
Each direction is motivated by a hypothesis about how a change would correct an evaluation error.
A failed candidate does not by itself refute that hypothesis.
ERA first checks whether the proposed behavior was implemented and affected the final judgment as hypothesized.
New diagnostic results guide further work on the implementation.
Results contradicting the hypothesis can end a direction; budget exhaustion alone does not refute it.
We test whether this continuation produces more sustained gains than restarting proposals under the same search budget.

Preference acquisition and ERA target two obstacles to learning an evaluation procedure from human feedback: redundant annotation and premature abandonment of corrective directions.
The cross-domain evaluation separates acquisition efficiency, search progress, and final evaluator agreement across structured visual artifacts, images and 3D, text generation, and automated research.
It includes reused and newly collected preferences, with a pointwise evaluator for each task standard.
The comparisons include evaluator fine-tuning \citep{kim2024prometheus,kim2024prometheus2} and score calibration under controlled feedback and observation access.
